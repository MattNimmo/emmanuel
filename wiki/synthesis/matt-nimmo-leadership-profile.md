**TLDR:** Matt is a worship director by formation who stepped into creative director 8 weeks ago. His leadership strengths are real and developed. His growth edges are mostly about integration — learning to lead *from* what he already carries rather than building frameworks around it.

---

## Context

Matt and JC both stepped into co-Creative Director roles at the same time, approximately 8 weeks prior to May 2026. Matt's prior role was Worship Director; he has been at Emmanuel since 2009 and on staff since 2019. JC's background is rooted in production and worship leadership.

This context matters for everything that follows. Neither is an established director. Both are calibrating their lanes. And Matt is crossing domains — from a discipline that runs primarily on spiritual attunement and relational presence (worship) to one that requires systematic management of production, communications, and creative output. What looks like tentativeness in some moments is partly appropriate 8-week orientation. But some patterns are deeper than that.

This profile was drawn from close reading of seven meeting transcripts spanning April–May 2026, including internal team meetings, director syncs, weekend planning, and external vendor consultations.

---

## What His Worship Background Gives Him

This is the most underutilized asset in his new role and the least obvious from the outside.

A worship director spends years developing instincts that don't show up on a production checklist: reading the room, sensing when something spiritual is happening, knowing what breaks the atmosphere and what sustains it. These are not soft skills — they're finely developed perceptual tools. And they translate directly into creative direction in ways Matt hasn't yet fully claimed.

**"Less is more during message"** — his clearest camera philosophy — is not a production principle he learned. It's a worship leader's instinct about what keeps people present. He can name it as production wisdom, but the knowing is older than the role. The same goes for his instinct around response moments, his read on what makes a service feel coherent vs. choppy, and his immediate understanding of what the locations need emotionally from what they receive on screen.

His relationship to testimony is also rooted here. *"Those are the stories that resonate with me for literally years afterwards."* This is not a content strategy preference. It's a worship leader's relationship to transformation — the belief that the primary unit of ministry is the story of what God did in a life. He carries people's stories because that's what worship directors do.

The integration challenge for Matt isn't adding new skills — it's trusting that his formation as a worship director is legitimate authority in the creative director chair.

## Core Character Patterns

### 1. The Insider Who Leads Like a Guest

Matt has seventeen years of relational and institutional capital at Emmanuel. He knows the organizational history, the pastoral preferences, the reasons past decisions were made, and the relational landscape in every room he enters. He is not a newcomer to this organization.

But across every transcript, he introduces himself — to vendors, to his own team, in his own meeting — through the frame of *"I'm new to the role."* This is true, and it's appropriate humility. But it has an effect: it consistently positions him as someone still earning authority rather than exercising it.

When he trusts his long-tenure identity — the institutional insider, the relationally embedded leader — he's confident, direct, and effective. When he defaults to the role-newcomer identity, he hedges, defers, and softens. The switch between them is not always conscious.

The practical insight: his authority in this role doesn't come from how long he's been a creative director. It comes from who he is to this organization and what he's been formed to know. These are not the same clock.

### 2. He Protects in Both Directions — At a Cost

Matt is one of the most naturally protective leaders in these transcripts. He protects his team from criticism above: when Josh's run-through performance is questioned, he finds the charitable explanation without dismissing the critique. When his comms team is burning out and receiving tone-deaf suggestions from senior leaders, he says *"sometimes we just have to interpret for him"* — absorbing the friction rather than routing it.

He also protects above from below: he receives his team's real frustrations, validates them, and doesn't escalate them. He holds the middle.

Both acts are genuinely caring. But there's a cumulative cost. **The team is naming real systemic problems** — absorbed work, bypassed processes, capacity exhaustion — and Matt is carrying them internally rather than moving them through the system. His team may eventually read his graceful absorbing as tolerance for the conditions that drain them. And Matt will eventually run out of room.

The growth edge is not confrontation. It's learning to surface tension *on behalf of his team* to the people who can actually resolve it — rather than managing everyone's experience of it.

### 3. He Already Knows What He Thinks — But Doesn't Say So First

This pattern is clearest in the ClickUp vendor call. Before the call starts, Matt already knows what he wants: the tool, the team size, the decision process, the budget threshold. He presents the conversation as discovery, but his thinking is done. He's gathering confirmation, not information.

The same pattern appears internally. When JC floats the Resi recording idea for Thursday run-throughs, Matt immediately says *"two birds with one stone"* — the connection was already there. When walking through Mother's Day service flow, he talks as if reading from a mental document he'd already prepared. He processes internally and presents externally as if he's still processing.

This creates a gap between his actual decisiveness and his perceived confidence in the room. His team experiences him as collaborative and exploratory when he's often already landed. More importantly: they don't get to experience him as someone who *knows things and leads from that knowing*. 

This is not intellectual humility (which is genuine and worth keeping). It's a communication habit that undersells his actual clarity.

### 4. The Speed from Vision to Structure

When something moves him, he immediately builds the container for it.

In the offsite, when the team is animated about 180 testimony videos, Matt says: *"I put building a culture around following the stories that Emmanuel tells us... I could just see people like, 'Did you see the new video?'"* — and within thirty seconds he's proposing a two-tier production format: quick reflexive version vs. long-form mini doc.

This is a genuine superpower. He converts vision into actionable architecture faster than almost anyone in the room. But it's also a compression. **He translates his deepest motivations into mechanisms before the people around him have felt them.** A team follows systems because they're required to. They follow a leader who is moved by something because they want to.

The version of this that would be more powerful: let the story land first. Stay in it for a moment. Then build.

### 5. Humor as Social Management

Humor appears consistently at moments of tension or self-exposure. Before introducing a potentially contentious topic: *"Can I open a can of worms? Maybe it's not a can of worms. Maybe it's a can of strawberries."* After something goes wrong: *"Not critically enough. That was a miss."* (followed immediately by deflecting to next steps). In the vendor call, after being put in a mildly awkward position: small joke to release it.

This is not a flaw. It's socially skillful and the team responds warmly. But it's worth naming because it functions partly as deflection from vulnerability — a way to ease past moments that carry real weight before they fully land. *"Are you sure you want to be on staff?"* said as a laugh line covers what was apparently a genuinely difficult period. The humor is real but it also moves the room past things that might be worth sitting in longer.

## The Co-Leadership Dynamic with JC

Eight weeks in, the Matt-JC partnership is still finding its shape. What's visible so far:

JC supplies the meeting's emotional temperature — vision language, team affirmation at scale, aspirational framing. Matt supplies the structure — agenda, action items, service flow details, next steps. JC is the floor lamp; Matt is the wiring.

Both contributions are real and necessary. But the dynamic carries a risk if it calcifies: Matt gets narrowed to the operational lane, and his instincts about service experience, storytelling, and encounter — which are genuinely distinctive — get underused.

There are also moments in these transcripts where Matt contributes something JC doesn't immediately have, and it lands well. The Resi recording idea for Thursday run-throughs. The *"less is more"* camera standard for message. The observation that the reasons for stopping united worship across locations *"wasn't because it wasn't awesome."* These aren't tactical points — they're vision-level instincts. He just delivers them quietly, in the middle of other conversation, without staking them.

The partnership needs Matt to bring his worship-rooted vision into the room with the same weight JC brings his production energy. That's not competition — it's completion.

## Growth Edges (Contextualized)

### Claim the worship director's authority in the creative chair
His formation is not a prior life — it's live competency. He knows things about service atmosphere, encounter, and presence that most people in these meetings don't. He tends to hold this knowledge in the background. It deserves the foreground.

### Start with what you know
The pattern of presenting settled thinking as exploratory is solvable. Begin with the position: *"Here's what I think we should do."* Then invite input. This doesn't close down the room — it anchors it. His team will orient better around a visible stake than around a question.

### Surface tension rather than absorb it
The protective instinct is genuine virtue. But the team's real frustrations (capacity pressure, process bypasses, unclear ownership) need to travel upward, not dissolve in his grace. His role requires being willing to say hard things in hard directions, not just absorb them.

### Let story land before it becomes structure
He has a genuine emotional core — testimony moves him, encounter matters to him, he wants his sister-in-law in the room. He has not yet learned to lead from that core in a way the team can feel. The mechanism-building is impressive; the source behind it is more compelling.

### Develop a distinctive point of view
The translator role is real and valuable. But a creative director also needs a voice the team can follow — a perspective that's specifically Matt's, not a synthesis of everyone else's best thinking. The raw material is there: the worship instinct, the testimony investment, the systems-as-elegance aesthetic. These are a point of view. They just need to be stated as one.

## The Integration Challenge

The next phase of Matt's growth isn't adding competency in unfamiliar domains (though that will happen). It's integrating who he's been formed to be with the new scope of what he's been asked to do.

He arrived in this role from seventeen years of being embedded in this church and five as a worship director. He has things in him — about encounter, about story, about what a service is actually *for* — that his new role needs more than it needs another systems builder. The systems he's building are good. What would make them great is if they were unmistakably *his* — shaped by a vision that comes from somewhere real.

Eight weeks in, he's appropriately learning. The question for the year ahead is whether the tentativeness releases as competency builds — or whether the frame of *"still new to this"* becomes a permanent way of holding back what he already has.

The evidence in the transcripts suggests the former. The capability is clearly present. What's still developing is the permission.
